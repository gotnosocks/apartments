Glossary

The terms on the research pages, in plain words.

Measuring a model

ELPD (expected log predictive density)
How well a model predicts asking rents it has not seen, added up over listings. For each listing, it is the log of the probability the model gave the actual ask. Higher is better. Only differences between models mean anything.
PSIS-LOO
Leave-one-out: predict each listing's ask from all the others. Refitting once per listing would take years, so Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) estimates it from a single fit.
ΔELPD and the ±
A difference in ELPD between two models on the same listings. On the frontier and the board, PSIS-LOO ΔELPD is against the simplest baseline model; in the held-out check it is against the reference model, so the two numbers for one fit differ. The ± is the standard error: two fits within about two of these are a tie.
Reference model
The PyMC model that was served before this research began. Held-out scores are measured against it so that every fit is compared with the same yardstick on the same held-out listings.
Pareto k
How trustworthy the PSIS shortcut is for one listing. Above about 0.7 the estimate for that listing is shaky; a few such listings are normal, many mean the score should be read with care.
Held-out check
Some listings (about 10%, from apartments listed more than once) are kept out of every fit. A fit's score on them is a genuine test, used to check that the PSIS shortcut ranks models correctly.
Unit split
A harder test: every listing of about 10% of apartments held out, so the model must predict apartments it has never seen.
Fit time
Minutes from start to finish to fit a model to the full dataset on one machine's hardware. Times only compare on the same hardware. The target: the full Chelsea + West Village dataset within 30 minutes on the RTX 2060 graphics card.
Complexity
How much a design asks a renter to understand, judged by the researcher and written down in rentfrontier.elegance. Each structural term (the market over time, a building's premium, a building's path over time, per-building slopes, unusual apartments) and each layer of facts (what the listing offers, what the ad says, the city's building facts, which streets the windows face, the neighbourhood) adds points. Terms that move over time or overlap another term cost more. Lower is simpler.

Choosing a model

The frontier
The fits no other fit beats on accuracy, fit time and complexity at once: anything better on one of them is worse on another.
Convergence checks
Whether the sampler settled on one stable answer. R-hat compares independent runs (near 1 means they agree), the effective sample size (ESS) counts how many independent draws the answer rests on, and divergences flag places the sampler could not explore. A fit that fails them is never served.
Servable
A fit the automatic selection may serve: it passes the convergence checks, has a PSIS-LOO score, ran on the target hardware within the time window on the full dataset, uses the current data rules and has a complexity rating.
Automatic selection
The rule that picks the served model from the servable fits: the most accurate, then among ties the simplest, then the fastest. It replaces the served model only when another fit clearly beats it.
Subset fit
A fit on a part of the data, used to tune settings quickly. Exploration only; never served.
Data rules
Fixed corrections applied before fitting, such as counting the same apartment's differently written labels as one, and leaving out listings a review found are not an open-market lease of a whole apartment.

Estimates

Estimate
The model's typical asking rent for that apartment, in that building, that month, made without the listing's own ask (leave-own-row-out). See how estimates work.
Below typical, typical, above typical
Where the ask falls among the asks the model expects for that apartment: the lowest 10%, the middle 80%, or the highest 10%.
