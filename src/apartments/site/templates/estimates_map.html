Chelsea and the West Village share one market trend in the model (one for each bedroom group: studio, one, two, three or more), and the neighbourhood itself only shifts the level. A neighbourhood grows faster or slower than the other only through its own buildings drifting from that trend, which the model estimates cautiously (each building's half-yearly walk, partially pooled across all buildings). So treat differences in growth between Chelsea and the West Village with care: the levels are firmer than the growth.

About this map

One dot per building on a map turned to Manhattan's street grid (avenues run up the page, the Hudson is to the left). Colour is how the building's typical asking rent compares with the median building that year, for the chosen bedroom count: blue cheaper, red dearer, gray within 5%. The chart below shows how the level itself moved.

The median building's rent is what a typical apartment of that size asks in the middle building of the area, features included. For a one-bedroom it is higher than the Estimates page's market reference, a stripped-down one-bedroom, mostly because real apartments and buildings have features that add to the rent. Intervals here are 90% (the range the rent is 90% likely to be in); most other pages show 95%, which are wider.

Buildings are shown in the years from their first to their last listing in the fit; outside those years their rent is the model's extrapolation (tick the box above to show them, hollow). Hover for the rent in dollars, click a building to open its page; zoom with the buttons or a double click, and drag to pan.
