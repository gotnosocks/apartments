Base model and defense case · 22 October 2026

The floor model of New York asking rents

Apartments project. Built from fits already run; no new fit, nothing served.

I would present the floor model as the base: a Bayesian hedonic model of 1.9 million StreetEasy rental ads in Manhattan and Brooklyn, with a city-wide market walk, a smooth rent surface for each borough zone, a partially pooled level for each of 67,977 buildings, unit and building attributes, and a floor curve for elevator and walk-up buildings. It is the model I can defend line by line. Park frontage is the one location feature I would put next to it, labelled as provisional.

The claim

For a renter comparing two apartments that differ in one measured quality, the model gives the difference in asking rent with a posterior median and a 90% interval. The numbers are strongest for qualities that vary within a building (floor, size, laundry, dishwasher, outdoor space), because there the building's own level is held fixed. They are weaker for qualities that vary only between buildings (doorman, condo, year built), because those compete with the building level and the location surface for the same variance. They are weakest for location itself: the surface predicts well, but named features of the city explain little of it so far.

The model

log rent_i = L(week_i) + S(day_i) market walk + 2 yearly harmonics + x_i·β bedrooms, baths, size, laundry, dishwasher, flags + f_g(floor_i) + flags_i GP floor curve, g ∈ {elevator, walk-up} + η_b(i) + ε_i building level, Student-t error η_b = F_z(s_b) + w_b·γ + u_b, u_b ~ N(0, b_z) map + building attributes + leftover F_z ~ GP(0, a_z² · SE kernel), HSGP basis z ∈ {Manhattan, Brooklyn + Queens} ε_i ~ Student-t(ν, 0, σ_bedroom group)
  • Target. Each listing's latest net-effective ask (concessions netted out), on the log scale. Furnished and short-term ads are excluded.
  • Data. 1,896,486 ads from July 2010 to October 2026 in 67,977 buildings, each joined to MapPLUTO by BBL. Training uses 1,706,837 ads; 10% of the latest ads of units listed more than once (189,649) are held out.
  • Zones. Manhattan and Brooklyn + Queens (the Queens rows are Ridgewood and Glendale), split at the river, each with its own rotated box, amplitude and building spread. No borough dummy.
  • Floor. Two exact Gaussian processes on log floor, pinned to zero at floor 1, capped at floor 47 (elevator) and 6 (walk-up). Penthouse, garden, lower level, top floor and floor-unknown flags sit on top.
  • Priors. Normal(0, 0.5) on coefficients, HalfNormal(0.3) on the building spreads and map amplitudes, an inverse gamma on the floor amplitude squared (median 0.15).
  • Sampler. Blocked Gibbs: the Student-t is written as a scale mixture so the location terms are conditionally Gaussian, and the global block is drawn with the building levels integrated out; 4 chains, 300 warmup, 1,000 sweeps keeping every 5th. Fit-only, under the project's 1-hour cap.

How it got here

Each step was a separate full fit, with written predictions before launch, and kept only if it beat the previous model on held-out ads with paired log scores and building-clustered standard errors.

What it says

Premiums below are 100·(eβ − 1), posterior median and 90% interval. Teal is the base model. Amber is the same model with each building's average unit features added (Mundlak), which removes the bias that building-level random effects put on unit features. Where the two disagree, the truth is most likely between them, and I report the pair as the range I'd defend.

Where the variance sits

Across training ads, the log rent variance is 0.192. Using posterior medians, the map carries 0.0436 (23%), the leftover building levels 0.0159 (8%), and the residual 0.0146 (8%). The remaining 62% is bedrooms, baths, size, floor, the market walk and their covariances. The descriptive share (map plus building levels) is 31%.

Location: what passed and what didn't

The project's rule is to move variance from the map into things you can measure. Every candidate below was fit as its own model and scored on buildings the model never saw, which is the only split that can test a building-level feature, since in the latest split each building's own fitted level absorbs it.

 Park frontage was fit on top of the six distances. Its gain is 2.3 SE and it was recovered in a fake-rent test (planted 3.46, returned 3.54, 90% 2.75 to 4.27, with no leak into the map). It still misses the framework's explained-share test against a within-zone shuffle (0.30% against a null 95th percentile of 0.46%), so I present it as provisional.

The six distances are the clearest negative result. They take 7.6% of the map's variance, their coefficients are stable across splits, yet they forecast unseen buildings no better: the map they replace correlates −0.97 with their contribution. At the 1 km scale the map already knows where the parks and the water are. A feature has to vary faster than the map to add a forecast, which is why park frontage, a first-row effect, is the one that helps.

Why this model and not another

  • Not the Mundlak model as the base. On simulated rents it fixes the unit-feature bias, but it cuts building features by 20 to 85% (condo 9.99 → 6.62 against a truth of 9.99) and worsens the map's recovery (level coverage 88% → 52% in Manhattan). It also changes what "alike" means for a building quality. I use it as a robustness bound instead.
  • Not floor plus all the location distances. They don't help a new building's forecast and they reassign map variance with names that may stand for neighbourhoods.
  • Not floor plus park frontage as one fit. That would be my choice, but no single fit has that exact design on the latest split. It is one fit (about 20 minutes, $0.80) when the fit hold lifts.

Questions I expect, and my answers

These are asking rents. Renters pay something else.
Yes. The target is the latest net-effective ask, which nets out advertised concessions but not negotiation. The claims are about asks. Checking them against independent records of rents paid is the next data step in the project goal, and I would say so on the first slide.
A hedonic coefficient isn't a causal effect.
Agreed. "Worth" here means the difference between two ads alike in everything the model measures. Unmeasured quality, such as a renovation or light, can load onto a feature it travels with. The within-building comparisons (floor, size, laundry) are the most robust, because they hold the building and the block fixed. Natural experiments are the planned route to causal claims.
Random building effects correlated with the regressors bias the coefficients.
We measured it. On simulated rents with a known truth the bias is 0.1 to 0.7 points; adding building averages removes it. On real data the building-average model moves size from 0.252 to 0.242, in-unit laundry from 6.3% to 5.7% and dishwasher from 2.0% to 1.85%. I quote both.
Your intervals look implausibly tight.
For coefficients they are too tight: in the recovery tests 69 to 81% of terms fell inside their 90% intervals, with misses of under half a point on terms whose intervals are 0.1 to 0.2 points wide. The intervals reflect sampling uncertainty given the model, not model uncertainty. The predictive intervals are calibrated: 90.4% coverage on new ads and 92.7% on unseen buildings.
With 68,000 building effects and a GP map, how do you know you aren't overfitting?
Partial pooling, held-out scores on each repeat unit's latest ad and on buildings never seen, and fake-rent recovery of the map (slope 0.99) and building levels (slope 0.78 to 0.89, shrinking for sparse buildings as expected).
Many fits were compared on the same held-out set.
Fair. Predictions were written before each fit and misses recorded, but adaptive reuse of the latest split is a real risk for small gains. The floor gain is 17 SE even with two-way clustering, far beyond that. Gains near 2 SE, like park frontage, are why the unseen-buildings split and simulations exist.
Why not gradient boosting?
The goal is the value of each quality, with uncertainty, not the best forecast. I have not run a boosted-tree benchmark, so I would not claim the model forecasts better than one.

Known weaknesses

  • Coefficient intervals are too narrow, as above.
  • Elevator units with no stated floor get one +4.0% flag that fits them slightly worse (−202 elpd on 15,272 ads).
  • Floors 47 and up share one column; the model prices floors 40+ 3.3% too high.
  • Held-out coverage is 0.799, 0.904 and 0.955 at 80%, 90% and 95% on the held-out latest ads. Coverage on a time-split refit for new units, which the framework also asks for, has not been computed.
  • In-sample PSIS-LOO was not run (fit-only default). Comparisons use held-out log scores, which are the stricter test.
  • No Bronx, Staten Island or most of Queens.
