Short paper · Revision 2, 22 October 2026

Asking-rent differentials for floor, amenities and parks: a Bayesian hedonic model of 1.9 million New York rental ads

Apartments project · Revised after three referee reports; changes listed at the end

Abstract

We estimate asking-rent differentials for apartment qualities in Manhattan and Brooklyn from 1,896,486 StreetEasy rental listings, 2010 to 2026, in 67,977 buildings. The model combines a weekly market walk, a reduced-rank Gaussian-process rent surface for each of two zones, a partially pooled level for every building, unit and building attributes, and a Gaussian-process floor curve. Adding the floor curve improves the held-out log score on 189,649 held-out ads, each the latest ad of a unit listed more than once, by 2,945 (SE 171, clustered by building and week) and lowers RMSE on log rent from 0.1172 to 0.1154, with 90.4% coverage of 90% predictive intervals. In elevator buildings asking rents rise with floor and rise faster with height, to about +4% at floor 10, +7% at floor 20 and +13% to +14% around floor 40 relative to floor 1 of the same building; in walk-ups they fall slightly. Identified from within-building contrasts with building means included (Mundlak), in-unit laundry carries about +6%, a dishwasher about +2% and 10% more floor area about +2.3%. A simulation audit shows that without the building means these unit estimates are biased upward by 0.1 to 0.7 points, and that posterior intervals for coefficients cover their truth only 69 to 81% of the time, so we report coefficients to the nearest half point. Distances to parks, water and transit do not improve forecasts for buildings withheld from fitting; frontage on a park is the one block-scale feature with a forecast gain, which is not yet established.

1. Introduction

Hedonic models price a good as a bundle of qualities (Rosen, 1974). The coefficients are equilibrium price differentials, not willingness to pay, which requires further structure (Epple, 1987; Kuminoff et al., 2013). For rental housing the bundle has three layers that are hard to separate: the unit, the building and the place. A doorman building on a park has larger, better-equipped units, so a regression that pools the layers credits the unit with value that belongs to the building or the block.

We estimate the difference in asking rent between two listings that are alike in everything the model measures except one quality. Unit qualities are identified within buildings, because every building has its own level. Building qualities are compared between buildings at the same point on a smooth rent surface; we present these as conditional differences, not as values. Location is the surface itself, and we ask whether measured features of the city improve forecasts of it.

The contributions are: (i) a model fitted to 1.7 million rows in about twenty minutes on one GPU, with 68,000 building effects and two reduced-rank Gaussian-process surfaces; (ii) a floor curve that depends on whether the building has an elevator, adding to the literature on vertical rent gradients in office and residential towers (Liu, Rosenthal and Strange, 2018; Danton and Himbert, 2018); (iii) a simulation audit of the bias that random building effects put on unit coefficients and of the coverage of coefficient intervals; and (iv) evidence that smooth distance features add nothing to forecasts for unseen buildings once a rent surface is present.

2. Data

The data are rental listings collected from StreetEasy between July 2010 and October 2026. The unit of observation is an ad; the target is its latest net-effective ask on the log scale, the last asking rent after advertised concessions. The last ask is the price after any cuts, so it partly reflects time on market. Furnished and short-term listings are excluded. Each building is matched to a tax lot (BBL) in MapPLUTO for year built, alterations, floor count and footprint. Manhattan has 21,994 buildings and 1,115,906 ads; Brooklyn and a small part of Queens (Ridgewood and Glendale) have 45,983 buildings and 780,580 ads.

Listings are a flow, not the stock. They over-represent units that turn over often and market-rate units, and StreetEasy's coverage of Brooklyn grows over the period. Rent stabilization is taken from the ad's wording, which under-reports it; asks for stabilized units mix legal and preferential rents. Our results describe the listed market and are not reweighted to the housing stock.

Unit fields come from the listing: bedrooms, full and half baths, stated size (44.9% of training rows; another 18.8% filled from the same unit's or line's other ads), laundry (in unit 29.0%, in building 36.8%), dishwasher (54.8%), floor and unit label. Building fields come from listings and city data: doorman (full, part, virtual), elevator, condo, gym, retail, height, year built, alterations, and frontage on a through truck route.

The latest split holds out 189,649 ads: for units listed at least twice, 10% of their latest ads, drawn at random. Held-out ads span 2014 to October 2026, like the training ads; each shares its unit with at least one training ad, and the median gap back to that unit's previous ad is 733 days. This tests prediction of a known unit's next ask, not a forecast: the market walk is interpolated through weeks that contain training ads. Some features also read other ads of the same unit or building, held-out and later ones included: size filled from the unit's or line's other ads (15.7% and 7.0% of held-out ads), building shares for elevator, doorman and gym, and the Mundlak means. No rent enters any feature, but these fills can borrow information from the future; a refit with features built from training ads only is among the follow-ups. A second split holds out 6,798 whole buildings and their 184,437 ads (the building split), the only one that can test a building-level feature.

3. Model

For ad i in building b in zone z,

y_i = L(t_i) + S(d_i) + x_i'β + f_g(i)(log floor_i) + η_b + ε_i, ε_i ~ t_ν(0, σ_k(i)) η_b = F_z(s_b) + w_b'γ + u_b, u_b ~ N(0, b_z²) F_z ~ GP(0, a_z² k_SE(·,·; ℓ_z)) (Hilbert-space basis, rotated box per zone) f_g ~ GP(0, c_g² k_SE(·,·; λ_g)), f_g(0) = 0, g ∈ {elevator, walk-up}

L is a weekly random walk with drift and normal shocks; S has two annual harmonics. The vector x holds cumulative bedroom steps, bath steps by bedroom count, log size relative to the median stated size for the bedroom count (with imputed and unknown flags), laundry, dishwasher and floor flags (penthouse, garden, lower level, top floor, floor unknown). The building level ηb is centred on the zone surface Fz plus building attributes wb. σ varies by bedroom group and ν is estimated. In the Mundlak variant, wb also includes the building's mean of each unit column (Mundlak, 1978; Bell and Jones, 2015). All coefficients are constant over the sixteen years, and the surface is static; Section 7 discusses both.

The zones, Manhattan and Brooklyn plus Queens, are split at the river. Each surface uses a reduced-rank Hilbert-space approximation (Solin and Särkkä, 2020; Riutort-Mayol et al., 2023) with the lengthscale fixed at 1 km, on a box rotated to the zone's footprint (the 0.1 to 99.9% quantile box, widened by a factor of 1.5). Manhattan has 5 × 17 = 85 basis functions and Brooklyn plus Queens 10 × 11 = 110; both resolve lengthscales down to about 0.9 to 1.0 km, so the surface cannot represent block-scale variation by construction. The floor curves are exact GPs on log floor, pinned to zero at floor 1 and capped at floor 47 (elevator) and 6 (walk-up). Coefficients take Normal(0, 0.5) priors; amplitudes and building spreads HalfNormal(0.3); the floor amplitude squared an inverse gamma with median amplitude 0.15. The fitted Manhattan amplitude, 0.92 (90% 0.77 to 1.09), lies in the prior's far tail (prior probability of exceeding it 0.002), so prior and data are in tension there; a fit with a wider prior is among the follow-ups.

We sample with a blocked Gibbs sampler. Writing the Student-t error as a scale mixture of normals makes every location and level term conditionally Gaussian. Each sweep draws the global block (coefficients, the walk's weekly levels and the surface coefficients) jointly with the 68,000 building levels integrated out, then each zone's building spread, the building levels, ν and σ with the mixing scales integrated out, the walk and surface amplitudes, and the floor-curve hyperparameters (the lengthscale with the amplitude integrated out). Every step leaves the posterior unchanged. We run four chains of 300 warmup and 1,000 kept sweeps, thinned by five. The fit takes 19 minutes on one A100. Because the sampler is Gibbs, HMC diagnostics such as divergences and E-BFMI do not apply; convergence is judged by R-hat and ESS.

4. Evaluation protocol

Each candidate term is a separate full fit with predictions written before launch; misses are recorded. A fit must pass a convergence gate (rank-normalised R-hat ≤ 1.01 and bulk ESS ≥ 400 on every traced quantity; Vehtari et al., 2021). Fits are compared by the paired held-out log predictive density (elpd) on shared rows, with standard errors clustered by building, and for the headline comparison also by listing week; a gain should exceed about 2 SE. Building-level features must also beat a within-zone permutation null on their share of explained held-out building residuals. Separately we refit the model to rents simulated from a fixed truth and score coverage of that truth, in the spirit of Cook et al. (2006) and Talts et al. (2018). Many fits have been compared on the latest split, so gains near 2 SE there should be read as provisional.

5.2 Floor

Figure 1 shows the two curves. In elevator buildings each doubling of the floor adds more than the one before above floor 2: 0.3 points from 2 to 4, 1.6 from 4 to 8, 2.6 from 8 to 16 and 4.8 from 16 to 32. The curve is not perfectly smooth: nine pairs of neighbouring floors dip by up to 0.6 points, floors 41 and 42 sit below floor 40, and all floors from 47 up share one value 3.4 points above floor 46. That step is largely the cap: the 9,105 ads in that group have a median floor of 52 (quartiles 49 and 58, maximum 104), and the curve's slope over floors 43 to 46 accounts for about 2.2 of the 3.4 points. Eight of the nine dips are under 0.15 points and not clearly real (posterior probability of a dip 0.54 to 0.71); the ninth, 40 to 41, is 0.5 points (probability 0.92) because floor 40 sits high. We read the shape below floor 40 and treat the top as unresolved. A penthouse adds about 8% above its floor's value and the top floor of an elevator building about 2%. Walk-ups decline by about 0.2 points per floor, to about −1% at floor 5. Adding the curve moves the elevator premium from 6.8% to 3.6% and leaves building effects almost unchanged (correlation 0.998). The floor differential bundles whatever travels with height in the same building, such as light, views, quiet and layout.

5.3 Unit qualities

We lead with the Mundlak estimates for unit qualities, because the simulation audit (Section 6) finds them unbiased where the random-effects estimates are not. Rounded to the nearest half point: in-unit laundry carries +5.5% to +6% against unstated laundry (random effects: +6.5%), laundry in the building +1.5%, a dishwasher +2%, and a private garden or outdoor space +2% (random effects +3%). Size has an elasticity of 0.24 (random effects 0.25), so a unit 10% larger than typical for its bedroom count asks about 2.3% more. A unit with no stated size asks about 4% less. The Mundlak fit has not yet been scored on held-out ads.

Within-building contrasts remove building and block confounding but not unit-level quality. Laundry and dishwashers probably travel with renovation, which varies unit by unit, so these differentials are upper bounds on what the appliance alone adds. A within-unit design using repeat listings of the same unit (Ambrose, Coulson and Yoshida, 2015) is among the follow-ups.

5.4 Building qualities

Between buildings at the same point on the surface, the conditional differences are about +15% for a full-time doorman, +10% for a condo building, +8% for a part-time doorman, +7% for a gym, +4% for construction since 2010 or before 1900 and +3.5% for an elevator; ground-floor retail is about −2% and frontage on a through truck route about −4%. Ads stating rent stabilization ask about 2.5% less and income-restricted ads about 10% less. With building means included these shrink by 16% (gym) to 56% (retail), and by 81% for building height, because the means absorb part of what distinguishes such buildings (Figure 3). These are descriptive conditional differences with no exogenous source of variation. Condo units in particular are rented by individual owners and differ from rental buildings in ways the model does not measure.

5.5 Location

Of the variance of log rent across training ads (0.192), the surface carries 23% and the leftover building levels 8%. Separating whether a place's qualities are priced (capitalization) from whether naming them helps a forecast, we address only the second here. A flexible surface will absorb any smooth amenity, so the test is whether a named feature predicts the rent of a building the model has not seen.

Six smooth distances (nearest station, routes served, any park, parks of 30 acres or more, shoreline, a noise index) take 7.6% of the surface's variance. Their coefficients are stable between the two splits and between single and joint fits; the largest is −2.1 points per log km from a park of 30+ acres. On the building split they change the held-out log score by +26 (SE 116), and at held-out buildings their contribution correlates −0.97 with the change in the surface (run building-zones-location-b0-buildings-35131df8-dense-w1000). The surface already holds these gradients at its own scale.

Park frontage is the best of the four, but the evidence is weaker than its 2.3 SE suggests. Most of the gain falls on ads that do not face a park (+135, SE 45), through shifts in the smooth distance coefficients; on park-facing ads the gain is +52 (SE 67), although their mean error falls from 4.3 to 1.6 points. It fails the permutation test (0.30% explained share against a null 95th percentile of 0.46%), the 40 m threshold was chosen after a check at 30, 40 and 60 m, and it is one of four features tried on one split. A fake-rent test shows the model recovers a planted step of this size (3.46 planted, 3.54 returned, 90% 2.75 to 4.27) without moving the surface. We report it as not established; a fit with park frontage alone on the building split is under way.

6. Simulation audit

We simulated rents from the fitted model under three scenarios and refit each: a realistic truth (the fitted medians, with the walk-up curve set to rise 0.4 points per flight, the elevator top-floor flag set to 3.9% and the lower-level flag to zero), a null (floor curves and features set to zero) and a planted building-height effect. All three pass the gate. Coverage of 90% intervals over 119 scalar terms was 80.7%, 75.6% and 73.9%, and 69% in a fourth recovery for park frontage. Each figure comes from one simulated dataset, and the terms are dependent, so these are indications, not calibrated estimates.

The misses repeat across runs in the same direction. In points of log rent ×100, the size elasticity comes back 0.6 to 0.7 high, in-unit laundry 0.4, laundry in the building 0.2 and the dishwasher 0.1 to 0.2. The null run's elevator curve, whose truth is zero, comes back 0.1 to 0.4 points above zero with 66% coverage. For size, in-unit laundry and dishwasher the bias follows the column's correlation with the true building level (+0.24, +0.22, +0.21), the bias of random effects correlated with regressors; laundry in the building has no such correlation and we read its bias as a spillover from in-unit laundry, since both are measured against unstated laundry. Adding building means reduces every unit bias to 0.03 points or less, but shrinks building attributes toward zero (condo 6.6 against a truth of 10.0) and worsens recovery of the surface. The surface recovers well (slope 0.99, coverage 94 to 99%, which is high because the truth is smoother than the prior), and building levels cover at 90%.

The truth was drawn from the random-effects fit, which favours its split of variance between building attributes and levels. Replicates with other seeds and with a truth drawn from the Mundlak fit are among the follow-ups.

7. Discussion and limitations

Asking rents and the listed market. We model asks, not contract rents, from a selected flow of listings. A comparison with contract rents from the NYC Housing and Vacancy Survey, and reweighting to the stock, are the most important next steps.

Interpretation. All coefficients are conditional differences. The floor differential bundles light, views and layout; appliance differentials likely include renovation; building differentials compare buildings, not treatments. Boundary and natural-experiment designs (Black, 1999; Bayer, Ferreira and McMillan, 2007) would be needed for capitalization claims about location.

Constant coefficients. Implicit prices are held fixed over 2010 to 2026, across COVID-19 and the 2019 rent law (HSTPA). Both plausibly changed the value of outdoor space, laundry and stabilized units (on regulation, see Glaeser and Luttmer, 2003; Autor, Palmer and Pathak, 2014; Diamond, McQuade and Qian, 2019). The surface is also static. A fit with period-specific coefficients is among the follow-ups.

Uncertainty. Coefficient intervals are too narrow: the audit's biases exceed their half-widths, and repeat ads of the same unit are treated as independent. We round coefficients to the nearest half point and treat the intervals as lower bounds. Standard errors of elpd differences are clustered by building only; weekly shocks shared across buildings are not accounted for.

Protocol gaps. In-sample PSIS-LOO (Vehtari et al., 2017) is not reported here. We have not benchmarked against a flexible learner. The data omit the Bronx, Staten Island and most of Queens.

8. Conclusion

A hedonic model with a level for every building and a smooth surface for each zone prices unit qualities from within-building contrasts at city scale on one GPU. Floor is the largest such differential after size, and its sign depends on the elevator. The model's weak points are measured rather than assumed: unit coefficients carry a small upward bias that building means remove at a cost to building terms, and coefficient intervals understate uncertainty. Location remains described rather than explained.
